# The final data story
[Addressing food acsess on CMU campus](https://carnegiemellon.shorthandstories.com/addressing-food-access-on-campus/index.html)

# Changes made since Part II
After conducting interviews in part 2, I realized there was a disconnect in my narrative. The original focus on the candy market to meet students' energy and sugar needs wasn't resonating with students. Two interviewees, who were students themselves, mentioned they rarely use candy vending machines, which led me to revisit the competition prompt. The prompt seemed to assume candy availability as a solution, but my interviews revealed that this assumption didn't align with students' actual habits.

Before analyzing the competition data, I recognized the need to identify the factors affecting our campus’s ability to meet students' energy and sugar needs. The key factors I identified were:

1. **Time Availability of Food**: Students at CMU lead very busy lives. I often joke with my parents that you can easily spot the campus stop by the fact that there’s always a student waiting for the bus—whether it’s 3 a.m. or 7 a.m. This reflects not only the hectic schedules students maintain, but also their varying lifestyles. For many, the operating hours of campus dining locations may not align with their needs. Vending machines are a step in the right direction for providing food outside regular meal times, but there are issues with the type of food they offer, as I later discovered. Another aspect of time is how long it takes students to reach food locations. With most dining options concentrated in three buildings—Tepper, CUC, and Resnik Hall—accessibility can be an issue. While opening new dining locations might be constrained by infrastructure requirements like kitchen sinks and heating sources, vending machines don’t face such challenges. However, the current number of vending machines on campus is limited.

2. **Cost of Food**: Graduate students, unlike undergraduates, don’t benefit from meal plans with discounted prices. They often pay retail prices on campus, which are generally higher than average off-campus dining costs.

3. **Dietary Restrictions**: CMU has a diverse student body with various dietary needs (e.g., Halal, Kosher, vegetarian). For some students, the available food options may not meet their dietary requirements.

4. **Healthy Food Options**: Candy is not an ideal solution for students' energy and nutrition needs. It’s not a meal, nor is it particularly healthy, as it can lead to glucose spikes and energy slumps. The current vending machines mostly stock highly processed, sugary foods, which are not conducive to maintaining focus during long study sessions.

By reevaluating the disconnect between the candy market analysis and actual student needs, I realized the narrative of my story needed to shift. This became the most significant change since part 2 of my project. A change in my design from part 2 was incorporating a color palette that represented the CMU colors, and the candy market theme.

## The audience
My target audience shifted to the graduate and undergraduate student organizations, as they are the primary advocates for student needs. They are well-positioned to connect with various CMU organizations and foster the collaboration needed to improve food accessibility on campus.


# Final thoughts
There are numerous directions to explore with this project, but one surprising challenge has been the lack of readily available campus data. 

- While the candy market data provides per capita candy consumption globally, I don’t want to simply highlight that the U.S. is the largest consumer. Instead, I aim to draw a comparison between the diverse campus population and the fact that many students come from countries with lower candy consumption rates than the U.S. This would support the idea that increasing candy availability on campus may not be the right solution. To do this, I need a demographic breakdown of our student population, which I’ve requested.
- I wanted to map the vending machines and dining locations on campus to visualize the limited number of vending machines and the concentration of dining options in specific buildings. However, I encountered difficulties in identifying the appropriate point of contact for collecting the vending machine location data. If I am unable to find the right person, I may need to either crowdsource this information or physically visit the locations.
- Having a dataset that details the meals and their costs at all dining locations would be valuable for analyzing cost as a barrier to food access, as well as identifying gaps in dietary requirements. Although this data is moderately available online, it is presented in PDF format, for which I ran out of time to parse into a usable dataset.
